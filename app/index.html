Checked, and left out

A deal finder earns trust by what it refuses to list. These were checked on Nov 27, 2026 and deliberately kept off the board:

  • Ninja Kitchen System with BlendSense (TB401), “28% off” at $179.95. Its Amazon price history (pricehistory.app) shows the same $179.95 on October 4 and November 10, and $146.99 earlier still. A routine sale price wearing a Black Friday badge.
  • Kindle Paperwhite (2024), “35% off” at $129.99. The $199.99 “list price” is the new 2026 model’s price; Amazon raised this model’s own list from $159.99 in August (Fortune, reported by TechTimes and MacObserver). The real saving against what this Kindle actually cost is about $30, not $70.
  • Amazon’s own-device percentages generally. Tom’s Guide notes Amazon raised list prices on some Echo, Fire TV and Kindle devices earlier in 2026, so discounts on those are measured against higher starting prices than shoppers saw last year.

How the checking works

  • Read at the store. Each price was read on the store’s own product page, or in a deals source that names the store, on Fri Nov 27, 2026. The card names its source and the store link opens the product.
  • The usual price is the store’s own. It is the regular or comparable price printed on the same page — never a price invented for effect. Where the “was” price varies (by color, for instance), the card says so.
  • Verdicts are evidence-based. “Verified low” needs a second source or a price-history check behind it. Conditions — a color-only price, a sale that only undoes a price rise — are printed on the card, not hidden.
  • Deal score, in the open. Score = discount % + savings bonus (1 point per $50 saved, up to 30) + verdict bonus (Verified low +20, Real with a catch +10, Mostly marketing +0), capped at 99. Best deals sit at the top by default; you can re-sort by discount, dollars saved or price.
  • Prices move. Black Friday prices change and stock runs out. Each card says when its price was checked and when the sale ends; the store page is the final word.
